01The setup

Two facts sit on top of each other and refuse to reconcile. Everything below is an attempt to work out which one is the mistake.

What the price says

Down −46% from its high and +4% below its own 200-day average — the deepest genuine de-rate on the board, and the worst drawdown in the company's listed history at −65% at the trough. It has been underwater since January 2025 — about 18 months, longer than any other name tracked here. This is not a panic. It is a sustained repricing of terminal value.

What the business says

Q2 FY26 subscription revenue +24.5% year on year, beating the top of guidance by 150bps. Full-year guidance raised by roughly $220M to $15.76–15.78B. Remaining performance obligation ~$29B, +22%. AI contract value crossed $1B. Renewal rate 98%.

The market has had about 18 months to be proved right. In that entire window the bear case has produced no fundamental evidence — it remains a claim about what AI will do, not what it has done. That is the central tension, and it can be read either as the market being early or the market being wrong.

02The bear case, at its strongest

Stated properly, because a thesis is only worth holding if it survives the best version of the argument against it. This is not a strawman — it is well-sourced and it is why the stock is where it is.

AI kills per-seat pricing. One person plus an agent does the work of three, so the vendor sells fewer seats. Enterprise software priced per human logs a headcount-linked revenue line into a world that is actively reducing headcount. Gartner projects AI agents will divert $234B of enterprise software spend by 2030. IDC expects pure seat-based pricing to be obsolete by 2028, with 70% of software vendors forced to refactor toward consumption, outcomes or capability pricing.

And the workflow layer is the most exposed part of it. If a company can point an AI at its own systems and generate the workflow app it needs, the argument for paying a vendor for configurable workflows weakens every year. ServiceNow's core is IT service management — routing, approvals, ticket handling — which is exactly the category of work agents are being built to absorb.

That case is coherent, and it is the correct default for most seat-based SaaS. The rest of this page is about whether it applies to this company. Three mechanisms argue it does not — one is decisive, one is strong, one is over-rated.

03The thing that would break first, and hasn't

If agents were eating seats, it would not show up first in the share price. It would show up in renewals — customers coming back for less. That is the leading indicator, and it is clean.

One honest qualifier: renewal rate is partly lagging. Enterprise contracts run multiple years and renew on a stagger, so disruption would appear gradually rather than at once. Rising contract duration can equally be read as strength (customers committing longer) or as defensive lock-in ahead of a weakening cycle. It is genuinely ambiguous and is presented that way.

04The pricing shift — the decisive mechanism

This is the strongest single fact on this page, and it is the direct answer to the bear case rather than a deflection from it.

Roughly half of new business is already off seats

In April 2026 CFO Gina Mastantuono said ~50% of new annual contract value now comes from non-seat models — tokens, connectors, usage. Now Assist is priced per assist: a small generative task costs 25 assists, a large agentic action 150. The full-year AI ACV target was raised from $1B to at least $1.5B.

Read that against the bear case in section 02. IDC says 70% of vendors will be forced to refactor pricing toward consumption by 2028. ServiceNow is already about halfway through that refactor. Under per-assist pricing, more agents means more revenue, not fewer seats — the disruption vector and the growth vector become the same thing.

What this does not mean. Fifty percent of new ACV is not fifty percent of revenue — the installed base remains overwhelmingly seat-based, so the transition is early and reversible. And consumption pricing is not a free upgrade: seat revenue is sticky and forecastable, usage revenue falls when customers optimise. Anyone who held cloud infrastructure names through the 2022–23 optimisation cycle has seen how fast a consumption line can decelerate. The company has swapped one risk for a different one, not for none.

05The security and governance leg

Real, expensive, and the most over-rated of the three arguments — including in the version that prompted this page.

Security & Risk is a 10-figure business in management's own words — $1B+ of revenue, one of the fastest-growing parts of the portfolio, and present in 16 of the top 20 deals in Q2 FY26. ServiceNow bought its way deeper in, fast:

AcquisitionPriceClosed

Veza — identity security, access across human/machine/AI~$1BMar 2026

Armis is the largest acquisition in the company's history. The strategic logic is coherent: a company can use AI to build its own workflow apps, but what it cannot easily build is a compliant, audited control plane across systems it does not own. AI Control Tower now claims to discover, govern, secure and measure AI deployed across any system, with 30 integrations spanning AWS, Google Cloud and Azure plus SAP, Oracle and Workday. Armis supplies what assets exist; Veza supplies who and what can reach them.

There is a second-order point that matters more than the revenue: Armis and Veza price off assets and identities, not seats. Device counts, OT endpoints, machine and AI identities all multiply as agents proliferate. So the largest deal in company history moves the revenue mix further away from the exposure the market is marking the stock down for.

Why this is the weakest of the three arguments anyway

Microsoft integrated Agent 365 with ServiceNow's AI Control Tower rather than routing around it, which reads as validation. It is equally the largest structural risk here. Microsoft has a long record of absorbing adjacent control layers once they prove valuable, and the governance-layer position is barely a year old and entirely unproven as a durable moat. If agent governance ends up inside Agent 365, this is the most exposed part of the business rather than the safest. It is also ~6–7% of revenue — a hedge, not a thesis.

06A popular claim that does not hold up

Included because it circulates widely, and because a page that only collects supporting evidence is worth nothing.

Claim: companies are ripping out SAP and replacing it with ServiceNow plus Workday.

Mostly false. Wholesale SAP replacement is uncommon — switching costs and operational centrality make it rare regardless of how unhappy the customer is, and SAP's 2025 NPS of 9 shows the unhappiness is real without producing the migration. What actually happens splits in two: Workday does displace SAP SuccessFactors in HCM (54 detected migrations to Workday against 19 back), but ServiceNow does not replace SAP. It runs an end-run around ERP — enterprises abstract existing systems through ServiceNow while leaving the system of record exactly where it is.

The reality is arguably better than the claim. Wrapping SAP monetises the customer without owning a multi-year migration, and it keeps paying whether that customer stays on ECC, moves to S/4HANA, or leaves. But anyone holding the stock for an ERP-displacement story is holding it for something that is not happening.

07What could still go wrong

The four ways this thesis breaks, in rough order of how much they should worry a holder.

01 ⚠  The record is from a regime that may have ended. Nine of its ten prior ≥20% drawdowns recovered — every one of them earned in a world where SaaS seat growth was unquestioned. A perfect record from a superseded regime is weak evidence about the new one. This is exactly how Cisco's dip statistics looked flawless until early 2000.
02 ⚠  Microsoft absorbs the governance layer. Today's Agent 365 partnership is the newest moat's single point of failure. See section 05.
03  Consumption revenue decelerates. The pricing shift removes seat risk and adds usage risk. Customers optimising their assist consumption would hit revenue faster and harder than seat attrition ever could.
04  Integration. ~$8.75B of cash for a business currently generating $1B+, spent while the market is punishing exactly this kind of outlay. Large acquisitions absorb management attention at the worst possible time.

And the uncomfortable meta-point: the market has had the 50% non-seat figure since April 2026 and de-rated the stock anyway. Either it is wrong, or it doubts the transition converts to durable revenue. That is not something this page can settle.

08Where this leaves it

Applying the same mechanical screen as the main board, and then the part the screen cannot see.

On the screen, it ranks near the top. It clears all three gates — quality tier, ≥10% off its high, below its own 200-day — and scores on the depth-matched rebound. One caveat is specific and important: ServiceNow has no days in its own history at its current depth that also have a completed forward year, so it is scored on the peer figure for that depth bucket. Across the quality names on this board, buying at ≤−50% returned a median +58% over the next year against +36% at −20/−30%, monotonically rising in between, across 14 calendar years and 13 names. Depth has been a signal, not a warning.

On tier, it sits at CONDITIONAL, and that is contestable. Its rebound statistics fall inside the PRIME band — between TSM and NVDA, both of which carry PRIME with negative worst-decile outcomes — and its 9-of-10 recovery record beats several PRIME names. Two arguments hold it back. First, every other PRIME name is a platform or monopoly mega-cap and ServiceNow is a ~$20B-revenue application vendor: a real moat, a different weight class. Second, its crash is idiosyncratic rather than macro — every PRIME name's worst drawdown was the 2022 rate shock or COVID, and a company-specific 65% fall is the shape a value trap makes.

The one number to watch

The renewal rate. If AI genuinely erodes seat-based software, it shows up there before it shows up anywhere else — and long before it shows up in the share price. Six years at 98% is the strongest single piece of evidence that the disruption has not started. A slip below ~96%, or net expansion cracking, would break the thesis on this page and should be treated as decisive. Everything else here is commentary.

Not advice — a mechanical framework plus stated reasoning. The central unknown, whether AI agents erode seat-based software, is unresolved and could reprice all of this at once.
