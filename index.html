01What just moved — the July drawdown, and whether it's a dip

Between 25 June and 24 July the AI-semiconductor complex shed roughly $1.5 trillion of market value across three separate shocks — a memory/AI-capex break, an EDA moat scare, and an earnings cluster. Then the late-July prints landed and much of it snapped back in two sessions, violently and unevenly. The table below refreshes daily with the market; the written read that follows is dated, and will age.

Off the high is not the same as cheap

The names that fell hardest are the ones that had gone vertical first. SK Hynix is −41% below its high and still +46% above its own 200-day average, up +540% in a year. Micron is −32% off — and still +59% above trend, up +618%. A 40% retracement of a parabola is not the 40% the rebound table is built on: that history is mostly real bear-market washouts, bought from below trend. The vs 200-day column exists to keep the two apart — green means the fall has actually reached the trend line, grey means only the spike has come out.

What happened — the drawdown and its snap-back, newest first

Jul 30 ⚠  The snap-back, and a violent split. Microsoft +15.5% in a session on Azure growth of 43% and a $90.0B revenue beat; Meta −8.0% as net income fell 14%, operating margin went to 31% from 43%, and the 2026 capex floor rose to $130B. The memory complex round-tripped almost the entire drawdown in a day — SK Hynix +25%, Micron +18%, AMD +13%, Marvell +12%, Intel +11%. Amazon reported running at a $200B annual capex rate.
Jul 29 ⚠  The earnings cluster — records, punished on timing. SK Hynix posted the best quarter in its history (revenue +257% to ₩79.3T, a 76% operating margin) and still fell ~10%, because HBM4 shipments slipped into later periods against ₩64T of expected operating profit. Jul 24  Second memory leg. Korea-led sympathy selling and de-risking into SK Hynix's print: Intel −7.9%, Marvell −7.2%, Micron −7.0% — while the S&P 500 closed flat.
Jul 23 ⚠  Capex rebellion, and Tesla. Alphabet raised AI spending and fell 7.1% on the print. Tesla missed badly — $0.33 vs $0.49 expected, operating margin 1.4% against 4.1% a year ago — and dropped 14.5%.
Jul 21  Relief rally. Micron +12.2%, Intel +8.6%, AMD +8.1%, TSM +5.5% — and it round-tripped within two sessions.
Jul 17  EDA moat scare. A Kimi K3 demo ran an autonomous 48-hour chip design on open-source tools at a mature 45nm node. Cadence −9.5% and Synopsys −7.9% the same day.
Jul 15–16 ⚠  Memory and AI-semis break — where it started. TSMC's outlook, a Bank of America note that AI chip pricing had run too hot, and reports Meta may rent out spare compute. SK Hynix −11.5% in a session, Marvell −8.7%, Micron −8.0%, Intel −5.8%. The Asian materials and packaging suppliers underneath them fell as hard or harder.

The read — temporary or structural

The most important fact is what didn't happen: hyperscaler capex was not cut — it was raised. The four big buyers are guiding to roughly $725B for 2026, up ~77% (Amazon now running at a ~$200B annual rate, Alphabet $175–185B, Meta a raised floor of $130B against a $130–145B range, Microsoft $120B+). What changed is that the market stopped paying for that spending and began demanding visible monetisation. That is a multiple problem, not yet a demand problem — and the event that would convert one into the other is a capex guide-down.

Where both tests pass

Applying this page's own order of operations — quality tier first, price second — the set clearing all three gates narrowed sharply through the late-July earnings cluster, and in the most instructive way: the market took most of it away. On 25 July the qualifying group was MSFT, META, GOOGL and AMZN. Microsoft then rose ~18% in a week on Azure growth of 43% and is now back above its own trend; Alphabet and Amazon re-rated with it. META is the only PRIME name left that qualifies — −29% off its high and −12% below its own 200-day, because it fell on its print rather than rallying on it. Every other PRIME name now sits at or above trend: MSFT +8%, GOOGL +9%, AMZN +16%, AVGO +7%, TSM +14%, NVDA +4%, ASML +17%, AAPL +11%. That is the framework working as intended rather than failing: names bought below trend are supposed to stop qualifying once they recover. The rest of the current list is CONDITIONAL — see the Rank column on the board for today's order, which refreshes daily while this paragraph does not. The names that fell hardest are precisely the ones the ladder does not yet reward — they have dropped a long way and remain expensive against their own trend, and two of them (Intel, Marvell) are FRAGILE, which this framework treats as SKIP at any price. Not advice — a mechanical framework.

Those four days have now happened, and they answered it. Capex was raised again — Meta lifted its 2026 floor to $130B (range $130–145B), Amazon is running at a $200B annual rate, and SK Hynix took its own capex guidance into the high-₩40T range. Demand did not crack. What got punished was timing: SK Hynix posted a record quarter (revenue +257%, a 76% operating margin) and still fell 9.6% because HBM4 shipments slipped into later periods, and Vertiv raised full-year guidance yet fell 17% — its worst day in 15 months — on project-delivery timing. The demand signal held; the revenue-recognition calendar did not. Nvidia's Aug 26 print is the next real test.

How Rank works — and what it deliberately excludes

A name is ranked only if it clears three gates: it is PRIME or CONDITIONAL (FRAGILE and AVOID never rank, at any price — the anti-Cisco rule), it is already ≥10% off its high, and it trades at or below its own 200-day average. That last gate is the one that does the work: it separates a name that has genuinely de-rated from one that has merely given back a spike and is still expensive against its own trend.

Names clearing all three are ordered by the depth-matched rebound — the median 1-year return measured only over days the stock was at least as deep as it is today. This differs from the Dip→1y column deliberately: that column pools every dip together, so a stock in the worst drawdown of its life gets judged partly by what happened to it at −25%.

Depth is not a tiebreaker here — it is the signal. Across this board, quality names bought at ≤−50% returned a median +58% over the following year against +36% for the same names at −20/−30%, rising monotonically in between. Those deep observations span 14 separate calendar years and 13 different names, so it is not one lucky episode. A ᵖ marks a name scored on that peer figure because it has no record of its own this deep — which is the right substitution, since its shallow-dip average would apply a mild-dip expectation to a severe one.

Two honest limits. This universe is 26 survivors of the AI complex, so the deep buckets never include a name that fell 60% and kept going — the peer figure is biased upward by exactly the outcome a dip-buyer fears. And the depth effect is real but noisy: of the 14 entry-years in the deepest bucket, 2 lost money (2011 and 2014) over the next twelve months. A peer figure also says nothing company-specific — it cannot see whether a particular business is being structurally disrupted. A rank is a screen result, not advice.

How to read the four rebound columns

Each answers "what happens after buying on a dip?", measured over every day in the stock's history it closed ≥20% below its own high — any depth (a day it's down 50% counts too; no upper cap), whether it's still falling, at the bottom, or climbing back. That's many entry days per stock (e.g. NVDA ≈ 1,770), so nothing is cherry-picked. Worked example — AVGO:

  • Dip→1y — the typical (median) one-year return across all those days, held for exactly 12 months from that day. AVGO +73%.
  • Win% (first number in Win% · worst-10%) — how often that one-year return was positive. AVGO 100% — every dip-buy was up a year later.
  • worst-10% (second number) — the bad case: the return in the unluckiest 1-in-10 entries. AVGO still +15%; contrast AMD −43%, PLTR −64%.
  • vs SPY — the same median minus the S&P over identical windows (strips the bull-market tailwind — the fairest cross-name number). AVGO +55% better than the index.
  • Recov'd — of its ≥20% crashes, how many climbed back to the prior high. AVGO 13 / 14.

03Why the fair metric matters

The earlier "trough → peak" numbers were unfair two ways — they cherry-picked the best subsequent moment, and "trough → now" just reflected where each name sits in its own cycle today. The fair metric fixes both: the median 12-month return from every day the stock was ≥20% off its high — same trigger, same horizon, all instances. What it shows:

  • Pays most and reliably: AVGO (+73%, 100% hit, +15% worst-case), META (+85%, +58% vs market), CEG, CDNS, and — notably — TSM (+54%, +30% vs market, up 87% of the time). "Cheap won't bounce" was wrong.
  • Explosive but brutal-tailed: PLTR (+81% median but −64% worst-decile), AMD (−43% worst-decile), VRT (+186% but concentrated in the 2023–24 cooling boom — a one-cycle outlier, not a base rate).
  • Doesn't pay: SMCI (+14%, the only name that underperforms cash from a dip) — the old "34× rebound" was an illusion of peak-picking.
  • SK Hynix beats Micron on the same test (+51% vs +42%, 91% vs 77% hit, +2% vs −22% worst-case) — the HBM leader is the higher-quality memory play, though both ride the same cycle.

The fairest single number is "vs SPY" — it strips out the bull-market tailwind that flatters everyone. By it, the dependable market-beating rebounders are AVGO +55, META +58, CEG +41, CDNS +36, TSM +30, SKHY +25, AMZN +23; SMCI (−2), MRVL (+1), VST (+7), AMD (+10) barely beat or lag. That pushes the list toward AVGO / META / TSM and away from the froth.

Peer-reviewed backing (Consensus): large firms that drop >20% in a month earn ~+12% excess return over the next 12 months — strongest in technology — while small / service-tier names drift lower instead (Benou & Madura, 2003). That is exactly the split this table finds: deep dips in large, moated tech mean-revert; the sub-scale AVOIDs don't. The literature's one caveat is horizon — one-day / one-week drops can keep falling (Cox & Peterson, 1994) — which is why the trigger here is a ≥20% level and the horizon a full year.

04When to deploy — ladder status & the catalyst window

The method is mechanical: don't predict the dip — let each name's own drawdown trigger the buy, back-weighted so the biggest adds land on the deepest, cheapest rungs, in deep-ITM LEAPS so an early entry can't be liquidated. This panel reads the live off-high against those rungs and flags who's in a buy zone today. The signal is quality-gated: only PRIME / CONDITIONAL names earn a DEPLOY — FRAGILE / AVOID dips don't reliably recover (the anti-Cisco rule), so they're SKIP even when deeply down. Dip→1y is the fair-rebound reward from the section above. Not advice — a mechanical framework.

05The two Elon names, reworked

Tesla — the Q2 print put a number on the catch

The non-car story is real-but-unproven: robotaxis are genuinely driverless and paid (monitor-free since Jan 2026, multi-city), and Q2-2026 was a record 480k deliveries (+25%). The earlier dismissal of it was too harsh — credit due.

Then Q2 earnings (22 Jul) showed the cost of it. Revenue beat at $28.24B, but EPS came in at $0.33 against $0.49 expected, operating income fell 57%, and operating margin collapsed to 1.4% from 4.1% a year ago — with capex above $25B and free cash flow turning negative. Automotive revenue grew 8%, but on price cuts and incentives. The stock fell 14.5% in a session. Management restated the robotaxi and Optimus ambitions without new dated milestones.

That sharpens the original catch rather than removing it. Roughly 90% of the ~$1.1T cap is optionality (the car business justifies ~$150–350B), and the Q2 P&L is the evidence that the car business is now funding the bets rather than earning its own multiple. Unlike the 2022–23 −74% — a growing carmaker that recovered — so little is car earnings today that if the bets disappoint, the downside is a permanent de-rate toward the car floor (−60–80%), not a bounce.

Stance: unchanged in shape, better evidenced — dip-buyable as a sized position for the Elon optionality, added aggressively only on evidence (2027 robotaxi revenue in guidance, verified Optimus work), not narrative. At −36% off its high and ~25% below its own 200-day, the dip is real; so is the reason for it.

SpaceX — now inside the dip-buy zone, with the unlock cliff still ahead

Now includes xAI (Grok) plus — via a $60B all-stock deal (Jun 2026, pending Q3 close) — Cursor, the ~$4B-ARR AI-coding leader. Moat is real — 34× ULA's launch mass, 10.3M Starlink subs vs Kuiper's ~380 satellites. Cursor makes the AI leg real, but at ~3% of the cap (all-stock, ~15× sales) it strengthens the story more than the price.

The price has done the work. At $108 it is −49% from its post-listing high — down from the ~$145 at which this page first called it "not a screaming dip" — and it has now fallen into the $95–120 band below. The narrative premium that justified the earlier caution has compressed with it; what has not changed is the supply overhang.
